How AI Learns to Predict

A visual guide to how ChatGPT-style AI actually works. No math required. No, really. Zero formulas.

Why This Matters

In 2017, Google researchers invented the "transformer" — the architecture behind ChatGPT, Claude, and every modern AI. At its heart is something called attention: the AI reads text by shining multiple spotlights on its notes, asking different questions from different angles.

Everyone assumed attention was a clever engineering trick — something humans designed through trial and error, like designing a faster car engine. We show that is wrong. Starting from just one rule — "what does it mean to predict text well?" — the math leads you directly to attention. It is not a design choice. It is the only thing that works.

This is like discovering that E=mc2 is not just a useful formula someone dreamed up — it is the only possibility once you accept a few basic principles.

A mathematician named Conal Elliott proved the same kind of thing for a different piece of AI: he showed that the two ways of computing derivatives (used to train every neural network) are not engineering choices either — they are the same mathematical object viewed from two angles. We found the same pattern in attention: it is not designed, it is derived.

Part 2

Keeping Notes

The computer keeps a notebook of patterns. The key rule: the notebook must work even if you tear the page in half.

Imagine you are the computer. You are reading a name letter by letter, keeping notes about what you have seen so far.

There is one crucial rule your notebook must follow, and it sounds weird at first:

If someone tears your notebook in half, the two halves must combine to give the same result as the whole.

Picture it: you read "ALEXANDER." I rip your notes in half. I give the "ALEXA" notes to Alice and the "NDER" notes to Bob. They combine their notes and get exactly the same result as your full notebook.

Why this weird rule? Think of counting marbles. If you split a bag of 100 marbles in half and count each pile, you get 47 + 53 = 100. Counting "works even if you tear the bag in half." That is not a coincidence. It is the property that makes counting useful.

For text prediction, this tearability property is what the math demands. A notebook that does not have it would give different answers depending on where you started reading. And that would mean it is not really capturing patterns in the text at all, just memorizing specific positions. (There is a 200-line formal proof of this, but the intuition is what matters.)

Part 3

What Goes in the Notebook?

The notebook tracks three things: how many letters you have seen, what kind of letters they are, and which letters tend to follow which other letters (a heatmap).

OK so what do you actually write down? It turns out there are three natural things:

Page 1: How many letters? Just a count. You have seen 5 letters. Simple. Tearable, because 3 + 2 = 5.

Page 2: What kind of letters? Think of each letter as having a "personality" — a position in a room. "A" stands in one corner, "E" in another, "Z" way off in the back. Page 2 tracks the average personality of all the letters you have seen. Names with lots of vowels pull the average one way; consonant-heavy names pull it the other.

Page 3: Who follows whom? This is the magic page. Every time you see one letter follow another, you make a mark on a grid. "L followed A" — mark the A-row, L-column cell. Over time, this builds a heatmap showing which letter pairs are common.

The heatmap is the notebook. And here is the beautiful part: it is tearable. If you build a heatmap for "ALEX" and a separate heatmap for "ANDER", you can combine them by simply adding the two heatmaps together, cell by cell. The result is exactly the same as building one heatmap for the full name "ALEXANDER."

This is not an accident. It is the reason the heatmap works as a notebook. Adding grids together, cell by cell, is the mathematical operation that guarantees tearability.

Part 4

Reading the Notebook

To make a prediction, you "shine a flashlight" on the heatmap. Each flashlight is an attention head. More flashlights, better predictions.

You have built up this heatmap of "who follows whom." Now someone asks: "You just saw the letter L — what comes next?"

You need to read the heatmap. But how?

One way (expensive): Look at every single cell in the heatmap and use all of them to make your prediction. If your heatmap is big (say, hundreds of cells), you need hundreds of separate "reading rules." That is a lot of rules to learn.

Better way: ask a question. Think of it like shining a flashlight on the heatmap. Your flashlight says: "Hey heatmap, which letters tend to follow things that look like L?" It shines on the heatmap from one direction and you see what lights up.

One flashlight can only ask one question at a time. But you can use multiple flashlights, each asking a different question:

  • Flashlight 1: "What follows consonants like L?"
  • Flashlight 2: "What follows letters near the start of names?"
  • Flashlight 3: "What follows in common pairs?"

More flashlights means better predictions, but with shrinking improvements. The jump from zero to one flashlight is huge. From three to four? Barely noticeable.

Each flashlight is making a "query" — asking a specific question about the heatmap. The heatmap stores information as "keys" and "values." The flashlight (the query) finds relevant keys and retrieves their values.

If that sounds familiar, it is because this is exactly the language AI researchers use. Queries, keys, values. We arrived at it just by asking "what is the best way to read a notebook?"

Part 5

This Is How ChatGPT Works

The flashlights are "attention heads." The heatmap is the "key-value matrix." Multi-head attention is just "reading a heatmap with multiple flashlights."

Let us connect the dots:

  • The heatmap is what AI researchers call the "key-value matrix"
  • Each flashlight is an "attention head"
  • The question each flashlight asks is a "query"
  • Using multiple flashlights at once is "multi-head attention"

Multi-head attention — the core mechanism that powers ChatGPT, Claude, and every modern AI language model — is literally "reading a heatmap with multiple flashlights."

And here is the remarkable part: we did not design this. We did not start by studying ChatGPT and reverse-engineering how it works. We started from one rule — the tearing rule — and the math led us here. Attention is not a clever invention. It is the only possibility.

What is wild is that we did not start with attention. We started with just one rule: your notebook must work even if torn in half. Everything else — the heatmap, the flashlights, the diminishing returns — fell out of pure logic.

What makes this special? A mathematician named Conal Elliott proved something similar for a different part of AI. He showed that the way we train neural networks (called "automatic differentiation") is not a design choice — it is forced by the math. We found the same pattern: attention is not designed, it is derived. Both are cases of "there is really only one way to do this, and the math tells you what it is."
Part 6

What the Math Does Not Explain (Yet)

Two things in real AI break the "tearable notebook" rule. They work great, but we cannot derive them from logic alone.

Real AI like ChatGPT adds two things that we have not derived from the tearing rule:

Competition (the "softmax")

In our heatmap, each cell is independent. Real AI adds competition: when one prediction lights up bright, the others automatically dim. Imagine if making one flashlight brighter forced all the others to get dimmer. This "winner take most" behavior makes predictions sharper. But it breaks the tearing rule, because dimming depends on what all the other letters are doing, and that changes when you tear the page.

Position stamps

Our notebook does not care about position. "A is the first letter" and "A is the fifth letter" look the same to it. Real AI adds a position stamp to every letter: "this is letter number 3." That lets it learn patterns like "names usually end with a vowel," which depend on where you are in the word, not just which letters you have seen.

These two additions are why real AI is better than what our logic derives. But here is the honest thing: nobody knows why they work so well. The algebraic story stops at the heatmap + flashlights. Getting from there to full ChatGPT requires some engineering leaps that the math does not (yet) explain.

"Surprise" is the average number of guesses the computer would need per letter (technically, it is called "perplexity" — the exponential of the negative log-likelihood). A surprise of 10 means the computer is choosing between roughly 10 equally likely options at each step. A surprise of 3 means it has narrowed it down to about 3 options.

The remarkable thing: our heatmap method, which we derived from the tearing rule, beats the standard neural network approach that was designed by hand. And it does it with fewer learned parameters (fewer "knobs to tune").

That is the power of starting from the right question rather than tinkering with answers.
